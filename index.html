Traditional Chinese Medicine, in one circle

The Body Clock Mandala

A map of the day as Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) describes it. Around the wheel run the 24 hours, with noon at the top and midnight at the bottom. Qi, the body's moving energy, is said to pass through twelve organ systems in turn, about two hours each. Each belongs to one of the Five Phases (Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water), and the day itself turns through the four seasons: dawn as spring, midday as summer, evening as autumn, night as winter. Read it as a way to notice your own rhythms, not as a diagnosis. Take what is useful now.

About this mandala

A mandala is a circle organized around a center. This one gathers the TCM body clock into a single wheel, with qi, the flowing center of the system, in the middle. It draws on classical Chinese medicine as it is taught today, and on the Five Phase (Wu Xing) theory that sits underneath it. Other schools and practitioners describe these ideas in their own ways, and this map does not replace them.

How it is built

The wheel is a 24-hour clock with noon at the top and midnight at the bottom, so the day reads clockwise like a clock face. The twelve organ systems each take a two-hour window in a fixed order (the horary cycle, zǐwǔ liúzhù): Lung at 3 am, then Large Intestine, Stomach, Spleen, Heart, Small Intestine, Bladder, Kidney, Pericardium, Triple Burner, Gallbladder, and Liver at 1 am. Each organ sits across the wheel from its partner twelve hours away, for example Heart at noon and Gallbladder at midnight.

The ring just inside the organs is the Five Phases. Metal, Earth, Fire, Water, Fire again, and Wood cover the clock in six arcs, because Fire has two sets of organs: the Heart and Small Intestine at midday, and the Pericardium and Triple Burner in the evening. Outside the organs are daily-life petals, a narrow band for the feeling or quality each organ is linked with, and an outer ring of the four seasons.

The day as a small year

The classical texts pair the day with the year: morning is spring, noon is summer, evening is autumn, and midnight is winter (Ling Shu 44). With noon at the top this falls neatly into place: spring on the left where the sun rises, summer at the top, autumn on the right, winter at the bottom. Yang, the warm and active side of things, grows from midnight to noon and yields to yin from noon to midnight, the same turning as the yin-yang symbol at the center. Earth, the fifth phase, sits at the hinges between seasons, shown as four small markers.

The organ clock and the seasons of the day are two different layers, and they do not line up one for one. The Wood organs, for instance, keep their hours late in the night, just before the day's spring begins. The tradition holds both at once: the organ clock follows qi through the channels, while the four seasons follow the sun.

What is tradition, and what is science

The organ clock, the Five Phases, and the pairing of day and year are classical Chinese medical ideas. They are a way of organizing experience, and they have been used for centuries. They have not been shown by modern research to work as stated, and in particular there is no good evidence that each organ peaks in its own two-hour window. What science does support is that the body keeps time. Genes in nearly every cell run a circadian clock. Cortisol rises in the early morning, core body temperature falls overnight, and melatonin begins to rise in the evening. The TCM clock and the circadian clock overlap in spirit, not in detail. Use the first as a lens for noticing, and lean on the second, and on your clinicians, for health decisions.

Going gently

Paying attention to your body can stir strong feelings, and for some people old pain. Go one ring at a time, and stop when you need to. Several windows touch on emotions such as grief, fear, and anger. These are described as parts of being human, not as faults to fix or as the cause of illness. If what comes up is heavy, look for support from a trauma-informed therapist. This mandala is for reflection and education. It is not therapy or medical advice, and it is not a substitute for a licensed practitioner. Waking at the same hour every night, pain, bleeding, breathlessness, chest pain, and changes that last all deserve a conversation with a doctor.

A note on language

Organ names in capital letters, such as the Lung or the Liver, name TCM systems of function. They overlap with the organs of anatomy but are not the same thing. Chinese terms are given in pinyin with tone marks: qi (qì), shén, jīng, wei qi (wèi qì). Translations vary from author to author; the wording here is our own.
