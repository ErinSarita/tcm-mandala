Traditional Chinese Medicine, in one circle

The Body Clock Mandala

Three wheels for the rhythms of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM). The Phases wheel goes to the roots: from Wuji, the boundless mystery, through Taiji, the first movement, and yin and yang, to the Five Phases (Fire, Earth, Metal, Water, Wood) and the two cycles that join them, one feeding and one taming. The Day wheel is the body clock: qi passing through twelve organ systems around the 24 hours. The Year wheel is the Five Phases in their five seasons, from spring to winter. Read them as a way to notice your own rhythms, not as a diagnosis. Take what is useful now.

Choose any ring or petal to open it.

About this mandala

A mandala is a circle organized around a center. This one gathers the roots of Five Phase theory, the TCM body clock, and the Five Phase year into three wheels, with the source, the flowing center of the system, in the middle. It draws on classical Chinese medicine as it is taught today, and on the Five Phase (Wu Xing) theory that sits underneath it. Other schools and practitioners describe these ideas in their own ways, and this map does not replace them.

How it is built

The wheel is a 24-hour clock with noon at the top and midnight at the bottom, so the day reads clockwise like a clock face. The twelve organ systems each take a two-hour window in a fixed order (the horary cycle, zǐwǔ liúzhù): Lung at 3 am, then Large Intestine, Stomach, Spleen, Heart, Small Intestine, Bladder, Kidney, Pericardium, Triple Burner, Gallbladder, and Liver at 1 am. Each organ sits across the wheel from its partner twelve hours away, for example Heart at noon and Gallbladder at midnight.

The ring just inside the organs is the Five Phases. Metal, Earth, Fire, Water, Fire again, and Wood cover the clock in six arcs, because Fire has two sets of organs: the Heart and Small Intestine at midday, and the Pericardium and Triple Burner in the evening. Outside the organs are daily-life petals and a band for the feeling or quality each organ is linked with.

Three wheels

The Phases wheel follows the Taoist account of how the many come from the one, in the order of the 11th-century scholar Zhou Dunyi's Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate: Wuji, the boundless; Taiji, the first movement; yin and yang; the Five Phases; and the ten thousand things. The phases are spaced evenly, Fire at the top and then Earth, Metal, Water, and Wood clockwise, so the generating cycle runs around the circle and the controlling cycle forms a five-pointed star, drawn behind yin and yang where all the phases meet. The texts give more than one route from one to many: the Tao Te Ching counts one, two, three, and the ten thousand things, and the Book of Changes moves from Taiji to two modes, four images, and eight trigrams. The panels note where readings differ.

The Day wheel follows the Chinese Medicine Atlas body clock: the twelve organ windows, the phase of each organ, a line for daily life, and the feeling each is linked with. The Year wheel follows Five Phase theory in its first home, the year: Wood in spring, Fire in summer, Earth in late summer, Metal in autumn, and Water in winter, with the organs each phase holds, the 24 solar terms of the Chinese calendar, and the calendar months. Seasons begin at the four starts (lì), around February 4, May 5, August 7, and November 7, so each solstice and equinox falls mid-season. Both wheels put noon and midsummer at the top.

What is tradition, and what is science

The organ clock and the Five Phases are classical Chinese medical ideas. They are a way of organizing experience, and they have been used for centuries. They have not been shown by modern research to work as stated, and in particular there is no good evidence that each organ peaks in its own two-hour window. What science does support is that the body keeps time. Genes in nearly every cell run a circadian clock. Cortisol rises in the early morning, core body temperature falls overnight, and melatonin begins to rise in the evening. The TCM clock and the circadian clock overlap in spirit, not in detail. Use the first as a lens for noticing, and lean on the second, and on your clinicians, for health decisions.

Going gently

Paying attention to your body can stir strong feelings, and for some people old pain. Go one ring at a time, and stop when you need to. Several windows touch on emotions such as grief, fear, and anger. These are described as parts of being human, not as faults to fix or as the cause of illness. If what comes up is heavy, look for support from a trauma-informed therapist. This mandala is for reflection and education. It is not therapy or medical advice, and it is not a substitute for a licensed practitioner. Waking at the same hour every night, pain, bleeding, breathlessness, chest pain, and changes that last all deserve a conversation with a doctor.

A note on language

Organ names in capital letters, such as the Lung or the Liver, name TCM systems of function. They overlap with the organs of anatomy but are not the same thing. Chinese terms are given in pinyin with tone marks: qi (qì), shén, jīng, wei qi (wèi qì). The Phases wheel uses traditional characters, as in the classical texts. The Tao Te Ching is also spelled Daodejing, and Tao is also spelled Dao. Translations vary from author to author; the wording here is our own.
